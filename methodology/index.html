Editorial standards

Methodology

How the tools are chosen, where the formulas come from, what the tests check, and what the site deliberately refuses to do.

What gets built

A tool earns a place only if three things are true: the task is repeated often enough to be worth a stable URL, the result is deterministic arithmetic or string processing, and the whole operation can run without a network call. That last condition removes a lot of otherwise reasonable ideas — currency conversion, live tax rates, sentiment analysis — and it is intentional.

The launch set is twenty tools across three categories: text, generators and calculators. There is no account system, no editor, no file library and no dashboard, because none of those are needed to count a word or convert a unit.

Sources for the arithmetic

The calculators are ordinary mathematics, but the constants and conventions are not arbitrary. Where a definition is standardised, the site follows the standard rather than a convenient approximation.

Units and conventions

  • Decimal and binary data units are separate. A converter that maps GB to GiB without saying which is which is wrong by about 7%.
  • Temperature is affine, not multiplicative. Celsius and Fahrenheit have different zero points, so the converter uses a two-step formula rather than a single factor.
  • Area squares the length factor; volume cubes it. The converter applies the exponent rather than reusing the linear factor.
  • Calendar months are calendar months. Age and date-difference results are computed with real month lengths, not 30-day blocks. A 29 February birthday is observed on 1 March in non-leap years, and the page says so.
  • Business days exclude weekends only. Public holidays vary by jurisdiction and are not bundled, so the tool states that limitation instead of guessing.
  • Loans use the nominal annual rate you enter. APR includes fees that are not part of the amortising balance; the calculator says this rather than implying its output is a quote.

Testing

The pure functions behind the tools — unit conversion, percentage arithmetic, date differences, amortisation, checksum calculation, random-integer sampling — are covered by automated tests with known values. Where a rounding boundary matters, the test asserts the boundary rather than a single convenient input. The UI layer is tested manually with a keyboard and with a screen reader, on a phone-width viewport and on desktop.

Tests are not a marketing claim. They exist because a calculator that is wrong in an edge case is worse than no calculator, since the output looks authoritative.

What the site will not do

  • No fabricated statistics. There is no “trusted by 2 million users”, no invented trending count and no fake review score.
  • No live-rate claims. Where a truthful answer would need a feed, the tool either does not exist or asks you to enter the rate yourself.
  • No medical, legal or tax advice. The calculators are arithmetic on your inputs, not professional guidance.
  • No dark patterns. No forced newsletter, no interstitial, no “download our app” wall between you and a result.
  • No copied media. Every photograph is a licensed Unsplash image with visible attribution; nothing is scraped from a competitor.

Accessibility

The target is practical WCAG 2.2 AA: a skip link, visible focus, semantic headings, labelled form controls, error text associated with its field, colour never used as the only signal, and a complete reduced-motion path. Motion is never required to understand a result.

Corrections

If a formula, a conversion factor or an article is wrong, the correction is published rather than quietly patched. The changelog records changes to tool behaviour, and article revisions update thedateModified and review status shown on the article page.

Send a correction with the page URL and the value you expected tohello@daygrid.app. A reproducible example — inputs, current output and expected output — is the fastest route to a fix.
