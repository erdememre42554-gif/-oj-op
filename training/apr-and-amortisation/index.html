APR, amortisation and why the last payment differs

The monthly payment formula is one line. The interesting part is what happens to the balance each month, and what an extra payment actually buys.

Editorial Team·10 September 2026·3 min read

A fixed-rate loan has one formula and one behaviour: each payment covers the interest that accrued during the month, and whatever remains reduces the principal. Early payments are mostly interest because the balance is large. Late payments are mostly principal because it is not. Everything else — the schedule, the total interest, the effect of an extra payment — follows from that.

The payment formula

For a principal P, a monthly rate r (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100) and n months:

payment = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n)

If the rate is zero the formula divides by zero, so the degenerate case is payment = P ÷ n. Any implementation that does not special-case zero interest will return NaN for an interest-free loan, which is a common bug in quick calculators.

The formula gives a payment that clears the balance exactly after n months, assuming every payment is made on time and the rate never changes. It says nothing about fees, insurance, or a rate that resets after a fixed period.

Interest rate is not APR

The nominal rate is the price of borrowing the principal. The APR is a broader measure that includes certain fees, spread over the life of the loan and expressed as an annual rate. Two loans with the same nominal rate can have different APRs if one charges an origination fee and the other does not.

This matters when comparing offers, and it matters for the calculator’s honesty. The DayGrid loan calculator uses the rate you type as a nominal annual rate. If you enter an APR, the monthly payment it computes will be slightly too high, because APR includes costs that are not part of the amortising balance. The note under the result says this rather than implying the number is a quote.

What the schedule shows

The yearly table is not decoration. It makes three things visible:

  1. The interest curve. In the first year of a 20,000 loan at 7.5% over five years, roughly 1,400 of the 4,809 paid is interest. By the fifth year the interest share has fallen by more than half.
  2. The balance curve. It is not linear. The balance falls slowly at first and then accelerates, which is why selling a house in the first few years of a mortgage often leaves little equity.
  3. The rounding tail. Payments are rounded to cents, so the final instalment is adjusted. Without that adjustment the balance would end at a few cents either side of zero, and the schedule would never terminate cleanly.

Extra payments

An extra monthly payment is applied to principal. Because interest is charged on the outstanding balance, reducing the balance earlier reduces every subsequent interest charge — which is why a small extra amount has a disproportionate effect on total interest.

Consider 20,000 at 7.5% over five years. The base payment is about 400.76. Adding 50 per month clears the loan in roughly 53 months instead of 60 and saves a few hundred in interest. The exact figures depend on when the extra payment is applied; the calculator assumes it is added to every scheduled payment from the first month.

Two caveats are worth stating plainly:

  • Prepayment penalties exist. If the loan contract charges a fee for early repayment, the saving is smaller or negative.
  • The last payment can be larger than the extra. If the remaining balance is less than the scheduled payment plus extra, the final payment is just the balance.

What the calculator cannot know

It cannot know your actual rate after a fixed period, your fees, your insurance, or whether you will refinance. It cannot know whether the extra money would earn more invested elsewhere — that comparison needs an opportunity cost, not an amortisation table.

What it can do is make the arithmetic transparent: the formula is printed, the schedule is shown, and the assumptions are listed next to the result. A number you can check is worth more than a number you have to trust.
