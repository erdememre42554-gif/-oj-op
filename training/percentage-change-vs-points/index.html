Percentage change is not percentage points

A move from 20% to 25% is five points but a 25% increase. Which number is correct depends on the question, and reverse percentages have their own trap.

Two statements can both be true and still sound contradictory: “unemployment rose from 5% to 6%” and “unemployment rose by 20%”. The first is a change in percentage points; the second is a relative change. Mixing them is one of the most common arithmetic errors in reporting, and it is not a rounding issue — the two numbers are different quantities.

The two formulas

A percentage point change is simple subtraction:

points = new percentage − old percentage

A relative percentage change divides by the starting value:

change % = (new − old) ÷ |old| × 100

For 5% → 6%, the first gives 1 point and the second gives 20%. Neither is wrong. They answer different questions: “how much of the whole moved?” versus “how much did the rate itself move?”

The rule of thumb: use percentage points when both numbers are already percentages of the same base. Use relative change when you are comparing two quantities of any kind.

Why the base matters

The same absolute change looks dramatic or trivial depending on where you start. An increase of 5 percentage points is a 100% relative increase if you started at 5%, and a 5% relative increase if you started at 100%. This is not a trick of presentation; it is what division does.

That asymmetry also means increases and decreases are not symmetric. A 50% drop needs a 100% rise to get back to the original. Losing half of a portfolio requires doubling to recover. Any calculator that reports “recovered” after a 50% fall and a 50% rise is reporting the wrong number.

The zero problem

Relative change from zero is undefined. If a value goes from 0 to 10, there is no finite percentage that describes the move, because division by zero has no result in the real numbers. Some tools return infinity, some return 100%, some return a very large number. All of them are inventing an answer.

The honest response is to say “undefined” and show the absolute change instead. The percentage calculator on this site does that, and the note explains why.

Reverse percentages

A discount is a forward percentage: sale = original × (1 − d). Reversing it is a division, not a subtraction:

original = sale ÷ (1 − d)

If a jacket costs 80 after a 20% discount, the original was 80 ÷ 0.8 = 100, not 80 + 20 = 100 — which happens to be right here only because the numbers were chosen to make it so. Try a 40% discount: a 60 sale price implies an original of 60 ÷ 0.6 = 100, but adding 40 gives 100 as well. The coincidence breaks as soon as the numbers are not round: a 90 sale at 30% off implies 90 ÷ 0.7 = 128.57, not 120.

The reverse formula also explains why a discount of 100% cannot be reversed: the divisor becomes zero, and no finite original price produces a zero sale price.

Stacked discounts multiply

Two successive discounts do not add. A 20% discount followed by a 10% discount leaves 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.72 of the original, which is 28% off, not 30%. The second discount applies to the already-reduced price, so its absolute value is smaller.

This is why “extra 10% off sale items” rarely means what shoppers expect. The order does not matter for the final price, because multiplication is commutative, but it does matter for how the saving is described.

A checklist before you publish a number

  1. Are both values percentages of the same base? If yes, points are probably what you want.
  2. Is the starting value zero? Then relative change is undefined; report the absolute change.
  3. Did you apply the rate to the original or the reduced value? Stacked discounts are multiplicative.
  4. Is the direction unambiguous? “Down 10%” and “up 10%” do not cancel.

The tool prints the formula it used next to the result so that the fourth check is possible without redoing the arithmetic by hand.
