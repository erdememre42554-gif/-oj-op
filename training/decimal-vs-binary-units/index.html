A kilobyte is not 1024 bytes, and a KiB is

Decimal and binary prefixes diverged decades ago. What the labels mean today, why an operating system reports a smaller drive, and why area conversion squares the factor.

The prefix “kilo” means one thousand in every SI context: a kilogram is 1000 grams, a kilometre is 1000 metres. For most of computing history, however, the same prefixes were used to mean powers of two, because memory is addressed in binary. The two conventions have coexisted for decades, and the mismatch is the reason a 1 TB drive shows up as “931 GB” in some operating systems.

Where the 1024 came from

Early memory chips had capacities that were powers of two: 1024 bytes, 4096 bytes. Engineers writing addresses in binary found it convenient to call 1024 a “kilobyte”, because 2¹⁰ is the natural unit of an address space. The habit spread into documentation, file systems and user interfaces.

The problem is arithmetic. Each step compounds: one “megabyte” of 1024 × 1024 is 1,048,576 bytes, about 4.9% larger than a decimal megabyte of 1,000,000. By the time you reach a terabyte the gap is close to 10%, which is exactly the discrepancy people notice on a new hard drive.

The standard that fixed it

In 1998 the IEC introduced distinct binary prefixes: kibi (Ki), mebi (Mi), gibi (Gi), tebi (Ti). A kibibyte is 1024 bytes; a kilobyte is 1000 bytes. The names are ugly, which is part of why adoption is uneven, but they are unambiguous.

Decimal (SI) Bytes Binary (IEC) Bytes Difference
1 kB 1,000 1 KiB 1,024 +2.4%
1 MB 1,000,000 1 MiB 1,048,576 +4.9%
1 GB 1,000,000,000 1 GiB 1,073,741,824 +7.4%
1 TB 1,000,000,000,000 1 TiB 1,099,511,627,776 +10.0%

Storage manufacturers use decimal units, because a 1 TB drive really does hold a trillion bytes. Some operating systems display binary units but label them with decimal names, which is where the confusion becomes a support ticket.

The converter on this site labels both, and does not guess. If you enter 1 GB and convert to MiB you get 953.674, and the factor line shows why.

Why area conversion squares the factor

Length conversion is a single multiplication. Area is length in two dimensions, so the factor is squared. One metre is 3.28084 feet; one square metre is 3.28084² = 10.7639 square feet. Volume cubes the factor: one cubic metre is 35.3147 cubic feet.

The intuition is a square tile. A tile one metre on each side is about 3.28 feet on each side. It takes 3.28 tiles along one edge and 3.28 along the other, so the same tile covers 10.76 square feet. The factor did not change; it was applied twice.

This is why “convert 5 square metres to square feet by multiplying by 3.28” produces an answer that is wrong by a factor of more than three. The tool’s area category exists precisely because this mistake is common in flooring and property listings.

Temperature is not a factor

Celsius and Fahrenheit are affine scales: they have different zero points as well as different step sizes. There is no single number you can multiply by. The conversion goes through a two-step formula:

°F = °C × 9/5 + 32
K  = °C + 273.15

A converter that treats temperature like length will return 0 °C = 0 °F, which is wrong by 32 degrees. The unit converter handles temperature as a separate case and says so on the page, because the failure mode is silent otherwise.

A note on rounding

Display rounding is not the same as arithmetic rounding. The converter shows six to eight significant digits for readability but keeps full precision internally, so chaining a conversion does not accumulate the display error. If you need an exact value for a contract or a specification, read the factor line rather than the rounded headline number.
